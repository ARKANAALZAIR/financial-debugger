# Valuation Frameworks

## Principle

Valuation method must fit the business model, accounting structure, and question.

## P/E
Useful for businesses where earnings are economically meaningful and sufficiently normalized.
Audit:
- cyclicality
- one-offs
- growth
- capital structure
- peer comparability

## EV/EBITDA
Useful in many non-financial operating businesses for enterprise-value comparisons.
Audit:
- capital intensity
- stock-based compensation
- leases
- working capital
- debt
- whether EBITDA meaningfully represents economics

Do not assume it is appropriate for every business.

## P/B
Often relevant for financial institutions and asset-heavy contexts when book value is economically meaningful.
Audit:
- asset quality
- ROE
- accounting quality
- intangible-heavy structures

## DCF
Useful when cash-flow forecasts can be bounded with defensible assumptions.
Audit:
- revenue growth
- margins
- reinvestment
- WACC/cost of equity
- terminal growth
- terminal value share of total value

Use sensitivity analysis when valuation is assumption-heavy.

## Sum-of-the-parts
Useful when materially different segments require different valuation methods.
Audit:
- segment definitions
- intercompany effects
- holding-company discounts
- net debt allocation

## Relative valuation
Use peers that are economically comparable, not merely in the same industry label.

Always ask:
`Is the asset cheap because the market is missing something, or because the business deserves the lower multiple?`


## Expectations audit

A valuation conclusion should distinguish:
- absolute business performance
- market expectations
- realized surprise

Strong results can coexist with weak future returns if expectations or valuation already reflect the improvement. Do not claim that news is “priced in” without evidence.
